Online blackmail: what it is, and what to do in the first hour

A calm handbook — not a scare page. What internet blackmail is, how it usually starts, first-hour steps, why paying rarely stops it, and how to refuse the second-wave “recovery” pitch.

What online blackmail is

People search online blackmail, internet blackmail, or blackmail online after a chat turns into a threat — or after a payment already left. The pattern is the same: someone says they will share images, a private conversation, or a secret unless you send money. The steal is usually gift cards, crypto, a wire, or a payment-app transfer — chosen because reversal is hard.

The “file” does not have to be real. A collage of your Instagram, workplace page, or family names is often scraped from public profiles. A claim that they “hacked your webcam” or “have your iCloud” is often a script, not proof of a breach. Shame is the product. The operator is counting on you to handle this alone and pay the same day.

Sextortion is the intimate-image form of this crime — nude photos, a video clip, or the threat of a leak. Online blackmail is the wider label. It also covers dating-chat secrets, a threatened “exposé” to your employer, and fake-hack emails that demand crypto to “delete the recording.” Romance fraud wants a long fake relationship. Blackmail skips that. Related hubs: romance scams · online dating scams.

How it usually starts

You do not need every step. Plenty of people pay on the first threat because the screenshot already felt final.

  1. Dating chat. A match or social DM moves you to WhatsApp or Telegram, then the tone flips — a leak threat, a “pay or I tell them,” or a demand for more images. Off-app rushes are also online dating red flags.
  2. Webcam or video. Someone starts a live session, records or claims to have recorded you, then messages a demand — sometimes within minutes. Dedicated playbook: webcam blackmail. Ordinary dating-video habits: video chat safety.
  3. Fake “hack.” An email or DM says they installed malware, captured your camera, or emptied your contacts. They attach a public photo of you as “proof.” The ask is crypto or a gift card “to delete it.” Treat the attachment and any link as hostile. Do not open them to “check.”
  4. Sextortion overlap. Intimate images or the threat of them. Same first-hour rules. Definition: what is sextortion. Longer action list: sextortion help.

You do not owe them a closing argument.

Silence is not agreement. It stops giving them new material and new proof that the threat works. Tell one person you trust instead of telling the operator.

What to do in the first hour

Order matters more than eloquence. Do not negotiate a deadline. Do not send more images “to prove you will cooperate.”

1. Do not pay

No gift card, crypto, wire, or “unlock” fee. Payment does not buy deletion. It tells them the threat works. If money already left, stop there — do not send a “final” amount.

2. Preserve evidence while the thread is open

Screenshot the profile, username, threat text, wallet addresses, gift-card brands, and URLs. Copy those into a note you control. Do not open new links they send.

3. Freeze the payment methods they named

Call the bank or card if cash left or if they have a card number. Lock unused gift-card receipts at the store if you have not shared the code. On an exchange, pause withdrawals and tell fraud it is online blackmail. Do not “correct” a payment they say failed.

4. Report the account, then FTC and IC3

Use Report on the dating app, social app, email, or video site — not only Block. Then file at ReportFraud.ftc.gov and IC3.gov. Same doors as how to report a dating scam.

Why paying rarely stops it

The FBI and consumer agencies are consistent: do not pay. Payment does not buy deletion. The file, if it exists, stays with them. A first send is treated as proof you will send again. A second ask — larger, same wallet or a new one — is the usual next step.

If you already paid, that is not a reason to pay more. Shame is why people wait and send a “last” card. Waiting is how a small gift card becomes a week of follow-ups. Call the bank, card, or crypto on-ramp the same day and say online blackmail or sextortion. Then file the reports below. Rail-by-rail notes: get money back from a scam.

Preserve evidence

Do this before you block if the thread is still visible. Apps can drop messages once you report. Deleting the chat to feel safer often deletes the only proof you have.

  • Profile URL or username, photos, and the full threat text — full-screen if you can.
  • Wallet addresses, gift-card brands, payment-app handles, and any URLs.
  • The original app or site, plus WhatsApp / Telegram / email if they moved you.
  • Bank, card, or crypto receipts if you already sent money.

Do not send more images. Do not open strange links after the threat. Do not install a “cleanup” app they send. The same freeze-first habit is in how to report a dating scam.

Where to report

The platform

Report and block on Tinder, Bumble, Hinge, Instagram, email, a video site, or wherever it started. Blocking without reporting only hides them from you.

FTC and IC3 (US)

ReportFraud.ftc.gov and IC3.gov. Use the words online blackmail, internet blackmail, or sextortion. UK: Action Fraud. Australia: Scamwatch / ReportCyber.

Local police

File a report if you feel unsafe, if images were posted, or if a bank later asks for a case number. Take the screenshots you have. You do not need a perfect file.

Optional image hashing (adults)

If intimate images are involved, StopNCII.org can hash files so participating platforms may help block re-uploads. It does not replace IC3. If the person in the images is under 18, use the NCMEC CyberTipline and local police immediately — this page is for adult victims.

If you are in the United States and need someone to talk to right now, 988 (call, text, or 988lifeline.org) is the Suicide & Crisis Lifeline — free, 24/7, confidential. If you are in immediate danger, contact local emergency services.

Watch for second-stage “recovery” scams

After an online blackmail incident — especially if you already paid — a new account may appear: a “takedown specialist,” a “white-hat hacker,” a fake FTC or FBI agent, or a firm that found your case and can wipe every copy for a fee. Treat that as the same operation or a copycat. They already know you are scared. People who already paid are the usual target of this second wave.

  • Unsolicited contact that names the incident and asks for crypto, gift cards, or a retainer.
  • Guarantees that they can delete files from every device or search engine, or recover the first payment.
  • A rush — “pay today or the packet goes out” — the same clock as the first threat.

The FTC is consistent: government agencies and legitimate organizations do not ask for money or financial details in exchange for a refund or a takedown. Anyone who does is a scammer. Official reports and platform tools are the path. Paying a stranger to “fix” blackmail is how a second loss happens. Broader warning: get money back from a scam.

FAQ

What is online blackmail?

A threat to share images, chats, or secrets unless you pay — also called internet blackmail. The steal is usually gift cards, crypto, or a wire. Sextortion is the intimate-image form. Do not pay. Save evidence, freeze payment methods, report the account, then file FTC ReportFraud and IC3. Definition of the image path: what is sextortion.

Should I pay an online blackmail demand?

No. Do not send money, more images, or a “final” payment. Paying does not buy deletion and usually brings a second demand. If cash already left, call the bank the same day and still file FTC and IC3 reports. Money steps: get money back from a scam.

What should I do in the first hour?

Do not pay. Screenshot the profile, chat, usernames, and any wallet or gift-card ask if the thread is still open. Then stop writing back and block. Freeze the rails they named. Report the account. US: ReportFraud.ftc.gov, IC3.gov, and local police if you need a record. Image-threat playbook: sextortion help.

What if they say they hacked my camera or iCloud?

Treat it as a script until a device you control shows a real breach. Do not open their attachments or “cleanup” links. Change passwords on a device you trust, turn on two-factor authentication, and still follow the first-hour list — do not pay to “delete the recording.” Video-session version: webcam blackmail.
